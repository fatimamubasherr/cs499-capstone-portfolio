01 Professional Self-Assessment

Professional Self-Assessment

How I think about
computer science now.

My computer science education changed the way I define a successful system. I entered the program thinking primarily about whether code could solve a problem. I leave it thinking much more broadly about architecture, people, data, trade-offs, security, maintainability, communication, and the decisions that exist behind every technical solution.

From writing code to designing systems

Throughout the Computer Science program, I gradually moved from treating software as a collection of individual programming tasks toward understanding it as a complete system. A solution can be technically correct and still be difficult to maintain, difficult to explain, insecure, inefficient, or poorly aligned with the needs of the people using it. My coursework taught me to think about all of those dimensions together.

Courses involving software engineering, testing, algorithms, application development, databases, graphics, mobile development, and computer science theory gave me different ways of approaching the same fundamental question: how do I create a computing solution that is useful beyond the moment in which it is written?

The capstone brought those ideas together. Instead of creating another isolated project, I returned to an earlier CS 340 client-server application and treated it as a system that could evolve. I evaluated its original design, identified weaknesses, made architectural improvements, introduced more purposeful algorithmic behavior, and strengthened its database layer.

“

The strongest version of a system is not simply the one that runs. It is the one whose decisions, structure, limitations, and value can be understood.

01

Collaboration, communication, and stakeholders

One of the most important changes in my thinking has been realizing that computer science is not practiced in isolation. Even when I am responsible for the implementation, the decisions surrounding that implementation affect other developers, users, managers, clients, and future maintainers.

Code reviews, documentation, written narratives, testing, and clear organization all contribute to collaboration because they make technical decisions understandable to people other than the original developer.

02

Algorithms, software engineering, and data

My work with algorithms and data structures strengthened my ability to think about efficiency and purpose rather than simply functionality. Search criteria, scoring, ranking, sorting, and pagination were introduced so retrieved data could be prioritized rather than merely returned.

Software engineering taught me to think similarly about structure, while database work added another dimension: data has to be stored, retrieved, validated, protected, and queried intentionally.

03

Building with a security mindset

Security is something I now see as an engineering habit rather than a feature that can simply be added at the end. Inputs, configuration information, database operations, permissions, error handling, and system boundaries can all introduce risk.

Developing a security mindset means asking what could fail, what could be misused, which assumptions are unsafe, and how sensitive information or resources can be protected before a problem occurs.

04

Where I am going next

The area of computer science that interests me most is the intersection of intelligent systems, software architecture, and real-world decision support. I am especially interested in systems that move beyond producing outputs and instead help people understand information, recognize patterns, and make better decisions.

My goal is to continue toward intelligent and cognitive systems while maintaining the engineering foundation demonstrated throughout this portfolio.
